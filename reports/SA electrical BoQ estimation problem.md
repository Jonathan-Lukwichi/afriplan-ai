# Unpaid take-offs squeeze South Africa's electrical contractors

> **Superseded for the deck (5 Oct 2026):** every claim used in the user-guide deck was re-verified
> against primary and peer-reviewed sources (Scite, Stats SA, CIDB, National Treasury, vendor pages) in
> [`docs/research/evidence-register.md`](../docs/research/evidence-register.md). Some figures below — the
> Irish "1 in 5 / 1 % per bid", the 93 % Grade 1–4 share, ECA(SA) member counts, unverified vendor prices
> and the Morena & Amoah ranks — did not survive that check and are not used in the deck.

South African electrical contractors win work by pricing a Bill of Quantities (BoQ) under a short legal deadline. They do it on margins too thin to absorb mistakes, using tools that were not built for SA electrical work. On consultant-led jobs the consulting electrical engineer usually writes the electrical BoQ. On drawings-only tenders, design-and-build and small private work, the contractor must measure the whole job from the drawings, and even when a BoQ is supplied, contractors re-read the drawings to check it. Public tenders stay open for as little as **14–30 days**. The only independent cost data comes from Ireland: each bid costs about **1% of project value** and contractors win about **1 in 5**. SA construction as a whole earned a **4.8% profit margin in 2024**, so one mispriced or missed item can wipe out a job's profit. The market is shifting towards small firms: **about 93% of the roughly 130,000 CIDB-registered contractors are Grade 1–4** (tender limit around R6m), and small and micro firms now earn **32% of construction income**. These are the firms least able to employ a dedicated estimator. The tools on offer fall into four groups: generic PDF take-off, US electrical estimators, AI symbol counters, and SA tender-pricing apps. None was found that reads SA electrical drawings (PDF and DWG), prices them at SA rates and checks a QS- or engineer-issued BoQ. The evidence has three blind spots. No study measures SA electrical take-off hours, SA tender hit rates or manual take-off error rates. Every "AI saves 70–90%" figure comes from a vendor.

## Engineers write the bill, contractors re-measure it in 14–30 days

**Who produces the BoQ depends on the procurement route.** Under ECSA's 2025 fee guideline, "calculation of quantities, cost estimates, cost control and the procurement process" are part of the consulting engineer's normal services unless agreed otherwise ([ECSA Guideline 2025](https://ecsa.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Government-Gazette-GUIDELINE-SCOPE-OF-SERVICES-AND-PROFESSIONAL-FEES-2025.pdf)). A public example confirms this: the electrical BoQ for Cabane Junior Secondary School on eTenders was "Prepared by: Evans Consulting Engineers cc", not a QS, and it calls the contractor the "Electrical Sub-contractor" ([eTenders electrical BoQ](https://www.etenders.gov.za/home/Download/?blobName=14e862e6-29fb-4e6b-b4c3-8648ed4cd173.pdf&downloadedFileName=Part+C+-+Electrical+BoQ.pdf)). JBCC lets lump-sum tenders be based on "Drawings and specifications only" ([JBCC Advisory Note 1.0](https://www.jbcc.co.za/advisory_notes/JBCC%20ADVISORY%20NOTE%201.0_%20Lump%20sum%20agreement%20Ed%206.2.pdf)). In that case the contractor's own estimator must measure everything. SA job adverts ask electrical estimators to "measure electrical works from drawings… (cabling, trenching, substations, transformers, poles, fittings)" and to "count electrical points and compile accurate quantity take-offs" ([PNet](https://www.pnet.co.za/jobs/electrical-estimator)). ECA(SA)'s tendering courses teach "measuring and extracting quantities" to contractors ([ECA(SA)](https://ecasa.co.za/training/estimating-and-tendering-course-eca-sa-cpt/)). A drawings-to-BoQ product therefore has two buyers: contractors who measure or check, and the engineers and QSs who write the bill.

**The bill follows fixed national rules.** The ASAQS Standard System of Measuring Building Work (6th ed., 1999) has its own Electrical Installations section. DBs, mini-subs, luminaires and socket outlets are counted "in number". Cables are measured "in meters classified according to type, size and method of installation", deemed to include "allowance for snaking". Connection to the main supply is a provisional sum, and earthing is an item ([ASAQS Standard System](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.asaqs.co.za/resource/collection/59AC5095-4BAB-481B-A973-427F24AC7003/standard_system_1999_6th_edit.pdf)). Real bills follow the CIDB "C2.2 Bills of Quantities" structure ([CIDB Standard for Uniformity 2019](https://www.cidb.org.za/download/100/procurement-documents-templates-and-guidelines/6157/cidb-standard-for-uniformity-august-2019.pdf)). They split into Preliminary & General, LV reticulation, electrical equipment and provisional sums, with separate supply and install rates. Each line traces back to a drawing type. Lighting and power layouts give the counts. Single-line diagrams give DBs and cable sizes ("as… depicted on single line diagrams"). Site plans give feeder, trench and sleeve lengths ([eTenders electrical BoQ](https://www.etenders.gov.za/home/Download/?blobName=14e862e6-29fb-4e6b-b4c3-8648ed4cd173.pdf&downloadedFileName=Part+C+-+Electrical+BoQ.pdf)). The legal layer adds priced items. Only registered persons may issue a Certificate of Compliance under the Electrical Installation Regulations 2009 ([EIR 2009](https://www.labour.gov.za/DocumentCenter/Regulations%20and%20Notices/Regulations/Occupational%20Health%20and%20Safety/eir2009.pdf)), and the BoQ prices "Provision of Compliance Certificates" as a P&G line. Wiring follows SANS 10142-1; ECA(SA) reports Edition 3.2 (2024) as current ([ECA(SA)](https://ecasa.co.za/news/sans-10142-12024-edition-3-2-released/)).

**Public tenders add a hard clock and a registration gate.** Contractors must hold a CIDB grading in class **EB (Electrical Engineering Works – Building)** or **EP (Infrastructure)**, or be registrable within 21 working days of closing ([CIDB SFU 2019](https://www.cidb.org.za/download/100/procurement-documents-templates-and-guidelines/6157/cidb-standard-for-uniformity-august-2019.pdf); [MarketDirect](https://community.marketdirect.co.za/portal/en-gb/kb/articles/cidb-grading-designations-and-classes-of-construction-works)). Bids are scored 80/20 up to R50m and 90/10 above ([PPR 2022](https://www.treasury.gov.za/legislation/regulations/PREFERENTIAL%20PROCUREMENT%20REGULATIONS,%202022.pdf)). Municipal tenders may close after just **30 days** (over R10m) or **14 days** (other) ([Municipal SCM Regulations](https://www.saflii.org/za/legis/consol_reg/mscmr435.pdf)). National and provincial tenders run a minimum of **21 days** ([Bowmans](https://bowmanslaw.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Public-Procurement-and-Government-Contracts.pdf)). Clarification requests close **5 working days** before the tender closes ([CIDB SFU 2019](https://www.cidb.org.za/download/100/procurement-documents-templates-and-guidelines/6157/cidb-standard-for-uniformity-august-2019.pdf)). In practice, quantities must be settled within one to three weeks, in parallel with site briefings, compliance paperwork and supplier quotes.

**The documents contractors price are often flawed.** A 2024 Wits study (20 interviews at CIDB-registered firms) found that "poor-quality tender documents compromise the accuracy of project estimates". It cites "inaccuracies and omissions in the bill of quantities" and drawing–specification mismatches ([Nezambe et al. 2024](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/383491282_Perceptions_of_tender_document_quality_and_its_impact_on_construction_estimates)). A survey of 91 CESA engineers blamed low design fees and "limited time available for checking and coordinating all design documentation" ([Akampurira & Windapo 2018](https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1021-20192018000300004)). The Cabane bill shows the problem in miniature. It contains duplicated item numbers, placeholder systems at zero quantity, and main DBs left as a provisional sum.

## An unpaid 1% per bid against a 4.8% margin

**No independent source measures how long an SA electrical take-off takes**, so any precise hours figure is a guess. Practitioners cite "4 hours per E-sheet" as an old rule of thumb ([Mike Holt Forum](https://forums.mikeholt.com/threads/time-taken-to-prepare-bids.64699/)), and "4 hours" for a house against "3 weeks" for a data centre ([r/estimators](https://www.reddit.com/r/estimators/comments/1b942nd/how_many_estimates_should_an_estimator_produce_in/)). Vendors claim 18–36 manual hours for a commercial electrical fit-out ([Aginera](https://aginera.ai/blog/electrical-contractor-bid-day-quick-estimate-from-drawings)). A defensible slide line is "hours for a house, days to weeks for commercial work".

**The economics of losing bids are better evidenced, though only from abroad.** An independent survey for Ireland's Construction Industry Federation found contractors won **21% of public and 27% of private tenders**. Tendering cost about **1% of project value per bid**, so roughly **5% of each awarded project's value** goes on bidding across the industry. Average public-tender margins were **3.8%**, and **56%** of respondents had bid public work at or below cost ([CIF/IDIRO 2022](https://cif.ie/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/IDIRO-Public-Tendering-Practices-and-their-Impact-on-Delivering-Value-in-the-Construction-Sector-Final9.pdf)). At a 1-in-5 hit rate, about four of every five estimates earn nothing directly. Making the take-off faster lowers the cost of every losing bid. No independent SA hit-rate study exists. The "20%" figure on SA tender blogs has no traceable source ([eTenderSA](https://etendersa.co.za/understanding-the-cost-of-bidding-for-tenders-in-south-africa/)). An older SA study of 160 Grade 5 emerging contractors found weaknesses in "tender price estimation" and a lack of the skills and resources needed to compete ([Seeletse & Ladzani 2012](https://scholar.ufs.ac.za/items/c52ba6bd-4578-46bd-81ce-e6d53d3eda74)).

**SA margins leave little room for error.** Stats SA puts the construction industry's 2024 profit margin at **4.8%, the highest in a decade**, on income of **R605.6bn** ([TimesLIVE reporting Stats SA](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/business/2026-07-09-stats-sa-highlights-decline-of-construction-industrys-role-in-economy/)). As an illustration (not a measured statistic), an under-count worth 5% of contract value, such as a missed feeder run or DB, erases the average job's profit. The causes are documented, even though the size of the error is not. In a survey of 74 SA construction professionals (45% QSs), **inaccurate quantity take-off ranked 8th** among causes of cost overruns, behind scope change. **"Accurate estimate" ranked 2nd among remedies**, and **reduced contractor profit ranked 3rd among effects** ([Morena & Amoah 2021](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1755-1315/654/1/012003/pdf)). An honest pitch therefore presents take-off accuracy as one lever the contractor controls, not as the main driver of overruns. No study measures a percentage error rate for manual electrical take-off.

**On the client side, producing a BoQ is expensive.** The SACQSP 2024 Guideline Tariff (gazetted February 2025) sets a basic QS fee of about **15.6% of value at R1m, 13% at R5m and 7.9% at R100m**. It allocates **40% of the fee to Stage 4 (Documentation & Procurement)**, where the BoQ is produced ([Gazette 52152](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/202502/52152bod741.pdf); [ASAQS](https://www.asaqs.co.za/news/694914/2024-Guideline-Tariff-of-Professional-Fees-for-the-Quantity-Surveying-Profession.htm)). By my arithmetic that is a ceiling of about **5–6% of value for BoQ production on a R2–5m job**, before the usual discounting ([Claasen & Cumberlege 2014](https://journals.ufs.ac.za/index.php/as/article/view/145)). Two caveats apply. The client pays this fee, not the tendering contractor. The tariff is also a guideline, not a binding rate.

## A R606bn industry splintering into 130,000 small firms

**Construction is a shrinking share of a weak economy.** It fell from **4.2% of value added in 2008 to 2.3% in 2025**. In constant prices it shrank from **R156bn (2016) to R99bn (2025)** of value added ([Stats SA](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?p=19701)). That is value added, not turnover; industry income was R605.6bn in 2024. Construction grew again in Q1–Q2 2026 on residential and non-residential building ([Stats SA](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?p=19893)). Large contractors have collapsed: Group Five, Basil Read and Murray & Roberts entered business rescue ([Stats SA](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?p=19701)), and Murray & Roberts Holdings was liquidated in 2025 ([FA News](https://www.fanews.co.za/article/surveys-reports-and-ratings/34/general/1195/south-africa-s-business-liquidations-surge-145-companies-closed-in-september-alone-year-to-date-total-hits-1180/42934)).

**Work is moving to small firms.** Small and micro enterprises earned **32.2% of construction income in 2024 (up from 25.0% in 2014)** and provided **55.3% of construction jobs**. The top-100 firms' share fell from 40.0% to 26.7% ([Stats SA](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?p=19701)). The CIDB register at 2024 Q4 held **108,224 active Grade 1 contractors and 21,331 in Grades 2–9**, of which 12,647 were Grades 2–4 ([CIDB APP via PMG](https://pmg.org.za/files/250625_DPWI_CIDB__Strategic_and_Annual_Performance_Plan.pdf)). That puts **about 93% of registered contractors in Grades 1–4**. Their tender limits run up to about R6m, a figure from a secondary source that needs checking against the gazetted values ([TenderProSA](https://tenderprosa.co.za/compliance/cidb-grading)). These firms rarely employ a QS or estimator. Estimating effort is roughly fixed per project, so it weighs most heavily on these small jobs. No public count of EB/EP-graded contractors exists. Nor is there an official count of registered electrical contractors: the Department of Employment and Labour registers them but publishes no total. The best proxy is ECA(SA) membership of **about 3,000–4,000 contractors employing over 75% of the trade's workforce**, and its own pages give both figures ([ECA(SA)](https://ecasa.co.za/service-menu/); [ECA(SA) LinkedIn](https://za.linkedin.com/company/the-electrical-contractors-association-south-africa)). Skills are scarce. The share of companies struggling to hire artisans rose from **10% in 2024 to 22% in 2025**, electricians are among the hardest to find, and the average artisan is in their mid-fifties ([Engineering News](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/the-talent-shortage-crippling-south-africa-is-in-the-trades-not-in-tech-2026-08-12)).

**Electrical demand is growing faster than construction overall.** Budget 2026 commits **R1.07 trillion to public infrastructure over three years**, including R217.8bn through provinces and R205.7bn through municipalities, the tiers where small EB/EP firms bid ([IOL, Budget 2026](https://iol.co.za/news/south-africa/2026-02-25-budget-2026-how-government-plans-to-spend-r107-trillion-to-meet-its-big-infrastructure-goals/)). Rooftop solar reached **8.3 GW by June 2026**, nearly three times the 2.8 GW of the REIPPPP and RMIPPPP programmes combined ([Engineering News](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/capacity-of-rooftop-solar-installed-in-south-africa-has-passed-8gw-mark-2026-06-19)). Market researchers count about 355 MW of data-centre capacity with about 185 MW more planned ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/03/11/3253598/28124/en/south-africa-data-center-market-investment-growth-analysis-report-2026-2031-featuring-key-dc-investors-africa-data-centres-digital-parks-equinix-microsoft-ntt-open-access-teraco-va.html)). No official figure exists for the size of the electrical tender market. A tender-aggregator's claim of "R15bn a year at R2.5m per contract" is unverified ([TenderProSA](https://tenderprosa.co.za/blog/electrical-tenders-south-africa-2026)).

## Four tool families, none built for an SA electrical tender

SA tenders run on an Excel bill. Bidders get a QS- or engineer-issued workbook with the formulas already in place, and handwritten rates invalidate the tender ([IDC tender BoQ](https://www.idc.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Bill-of-Quantities.pdf)). ECA(SA)'s own list of "essential apps" for electricians includes no take-off or estimating software at all ([ECA(SA)](https://ecasa.co.za/member-support/look-here-are-some-essential-apps-to-aid-south-african-electricians/)). No survey shows how many SA contractors still measure by hand, so "manual is the norm" is an inference to confirm through interviews. The rand prices below are conversions at R17.9/USD, not local list prices.

| Tool family | Examples | Reported cost (≈ ZAR/yr) | Where it falls short for SA electrical work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Generic take-off / QS | Bluebeam, PlanSwift, CostX, WinQS + DimensionX | R4,650 (Bluebeam Basics) to ~R36k; CostX ~R80k one-time | Measures counts and lengths, but a person still clicks every symbol and prices in Excel. No electrical logic was found ([Bluebeam](https://www.bluebeam.com/pricing/); [SelectHub](https://www.selecthub.com/p/takeoff-software/planswift/); [costx.co.za](https://costx.co.za/index.php/itwo-costx-2d/); [WinQS](https://winqs.co.za/)) |
| US electrical estimators | Trimble Accubid, McCormick, ConEst | ~R25k to R270k per seat, plus implementation | Built on NECA (US) labour units and US product databases. No rand pricing or SANS logic was found ([ITQlick](https://www.itqlick.com/compare/accubid-for-electrical-contractors/mccormick-estimating-software); [Construction Owners](https://www.constructionowners.com/news/neca-releases-updated-labor-reference-for-electrical-estimating)) |
| AI take-off | Togal, Kreo Pro, Beam AI, Autodesk Takeoff | R37.6k (Kreo) to R64.2k (Togal) per user; Beam ~R143k–448k per trade | Counts symbols on PDFs; a person must review the results. Vendors admit weakness on cable routing, one-line diagrams and pricing. Beam accepts PDF only and takes 24–72 hours ([Togal](https://www.togal.ai/pricing); [Kreo](https://www.kreo.net/pricing); [Beam AI](https://www.ibeam.ai/subcontractors/electrical); [Civils.ai](https://civils.ai/blog/ai-for-electrical-takeoffs/)) |
| SA tender / admin apps | TenderSmart, TenderProSA, Omni Field | TenderSmart from R699/month | Price or manage an existing BoQ, or handle CoC paperwork. No evidence any of them reads drawings ([TenderSmart](https://www.tendersmart.co.za/blog/what-is-a-boq-and-its-importance-in-building-projects-south-africa-2026); [TenderProSA](https://tenderprosa.co.za/blog/electrical-tenders-south-africa-2026); [Omni Field](https://omnifield.africa/industries/electrical)) |

AI vendors' own blogs concede where AI is weakest. "AI helps less with conduit and wire, as routing is not only detection", and one-line (riser) diagrams that show topology rather than true lengths "remain a known limitation" ([Civils.ai](https://civils.ai/blog/ai-for-electrical-takeoffs/)). Even optimistic vendors leave "pricing decisions, labour factors, code-compliance verification" with the estimator ([Aginera](https://aginera.ai/blog/ai-electrical-assemblies-2d-plans-beyond-symbol-counting)). That matters because counting sockets on a floor plan is the cheapest part of an electrical BoQ. The expensive, error-prone parts are feeder lengths off the site plan, cable sizes from the single-line diagram and DB build-ups, and these are the parts the vendors hand back to a person. USD pricing adds currency risk: the rand moved between R16.57 and R19.74 to the dollar in 2025 ([exchange-rates.org](https://www.exchange-rates.org/exchange-rate-history/usd-zar-2025)). This review found no tool that reads a DWG set and a PDF set of the same project together, applies SA rates, or audits a supplied BoQ for arithmetic errors and missing items. That is an absence-of-evidence finding, not proof that no such tool exists.

Two figures should stay off the slides entirely. One is the claim that "ASAQS says pricing errors are the #1 reason contractors fail", which traces to a blog with no ASAQS source. ## Conclusion

The defensible problem statement is about economics, not hours saved. Contractors work under statutory deadlines on a margin of about 5%, and most of their estimating effort goes on bids they lose, so the cost of a take-off matters more than its duration. The market is fragmenting into small Grade 1–4 firms that cannot carry an estimator or a R40–60k USD-priced AI seat. The high-risk items are the ones existing tools hand back to a person: feeder lengths from site plans, single-line diagram content, SA pricing and checking a supplied BoQ. Those are exactly the jobs AfriPlan's PDF and DWG/DXF readers and its BoQ audit are built for. Against vendor claims, that positions AfriPlan as complementary to symbol counters rather than a copy of them.

The weakest links in the pitch are also its biggest opportunity. No independent source, SA or international, measures electrical take-off hours, SA hit rates or manual take-off error rates, and no one has independently tested AI take-off on 2D electrical drawings. AfriPlan already scores its bills against human-priced reference projects and can time each run. Publishing those first-party measurements, alongside a short survey of pilot contractors on hours per tender and win rates, would turn the gaps that competitors fill with marketing claims into evidence that only AfriPlan holds.
